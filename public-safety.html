Help where there is no signal

In preparation.

The situation

An earthquake. A building is down, and so is the network.

A person is trapped inside. Rescuers work the rubble in teams, out of sight of each other, and every phone shows no signal. Nobody can talk, say where they are, or call for help. The same happens in basements, on overseas deployments and when a network is overloaded in a major incident.

12Awareness and understanding

The team’s phones keep working, because they never needed the network.

Voice, SOS, positions and messages run directly between the phones: D2D using Direct Mode Operation (DMO).

Today: a trapped person’s phone opens a voice and data channel. Rescuers can hear and answer them.

4Warning

It tells the rescuers and the command post.

Today, a person presses SOS and the call for help goes out with its position. Teammates talk in their own private group, where an SOS tells them who is calling and where; a phone outside the group hears nothing.

Today: one phone in between passes voice and data on to the field command post.

Today: data travels on from phone to phone. Voice passes through one phone in between.

Who is told: the team, the rescuers nearby and the command post. Nobody else.

What you get

Your team keeps its voice, its “I am here” and its call for help where the network is gone, on the phones they already carry.

Built for first responders (police, ambulance, rescue teams and the armed forces), for utilities and field crews, and for a person in distress or trapped. It complements existing radios and networks.

How we label what we claim

Questions evaluators ask

How far?
Reaches significantly farther than standard device-to-device (D2D) links. We state no range figure: walls, floors and bodies decide reception, not distance. Your pilot measures it.
Who can hear it?
Only phones in the group.
Can two people talk at once?
No. It is push-to-talk: one talker at a time.
